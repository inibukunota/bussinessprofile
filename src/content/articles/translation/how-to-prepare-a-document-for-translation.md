Translation quality depends on more than the translator's skill — it also depends on what they're given to work with. A document that's clear, complete and clearly marked up front tends to come back faster and more accurate than one that arrives as a rushed scan with half a page cut off.

## Send the clearest version you have

Where possible, send an editable file (Word, Google Docs) rather than a photo or scanned PDF. If a scan is all you have, make sure every page is fully visible, right-side up, and legible — blurry or cropped text is often the single biggest cause of delay, since it has to be clarified before translation can even start.

## Flag anything unusual up front

Say so before work begins if the document contains:

- Names, addresses, or technical terms that shouldn't be translated
- Handwritten sections or annotations
- Content that needs to match a specific existing template or format

None of these are a problem — they just need to be known in advance rather than discovered partway through.

## Mention the purpose of the translation

A document translated for internal reference can read more literally. One that's going to an embassy, a court, or a client reads differently — it needs to match the tone and conventions that reader expects. Telling us where the document is going helps shape how it's translated, not just what it says.

> The goal isn't to translate every word — it's to make sure the reader on the other side understands exactly what the original was trying to say.

## Set a realistic timeline

Short, standard documents can usually turn around quickly. Longer or more technical material needs more time, particularly if it requires a review pass afterward. If you have a hard deadline — a submission date, an appointment — mention it as early as possible so it can be planned around properly.

## The short version

Send a clear, complete document. Flag anything unusual. Say where it's going and when you need it. That's most of what separates a smooth translation from a slow one.
